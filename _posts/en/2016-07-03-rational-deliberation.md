This is the first piece in the series, so what should it be about? Let's start
with a behavioural test.

> You have a box of fruit. It has been sitting there for a month and you only
> remembered it today. When you open it, more than half of the fruit is close
> to spoiling, and the rest does not look much better. You have two options
> (and of course we are thrifty people):
>
> 1. Eat the ones that are about to spoil. Nothing gets thrown away, but you
>    may spend the next week eating fruit that hovers somewhere between good
>    and bad — because by tomorrow the ones that already looked doubtful will
>    have got worse.
> 2. Eat the good ones first. Some of today's nearly spoiled fruit will
>    inevitably have to go in the bin tomorrow, but at least the taste is
>    guaranteed.

Which would you choose?

This is a well-known experimental design by the psychologist Fick Teister, and
the result, in short, runs like this. Subjects who choose the first option tend
to have a higher tolerance for the status quo and are more tenacious in their
attachments. But because they may cherish everything they have ever owned a
little too much, they never quite reach the summit of their own needs, and
carry with them a permanent regret at never having stood on the peak to see all
the smaller mountains below. Those who choose the second have far more
decisiveness, but may act on impulse and miss opportunities. Who knows —
perhaps the fruit that is about to spoil is still beautiful on the inside.

What I actually want to say today is this: it is remarkably easy to fabricate a
test that looks like it holds up. To make it feel more real, the author can
throw in dates, places, sample sizes, case studies. And most people, given the
choice between forwarding and verifying, will — I suspect — lean towards the
former, as long as it sounds like it makes a certain amount of sense.

So from this article onward, I sincerely hope we can treat this like a table of
players in a game of Werewolf. For anything I discuss here — fiction aside —
please argue from pure logic. Let us look together at the causes and the
correlations behind an event, and not believe anything simply because an
authority or a well-turned principle was invoked. On the same grounds, I have
no patience for comments that come without reasons.

Consider this the opening. More will follow, slowly.
