I was watching a programme in the evening and it quoted an old saying: *before
you learn to do things, learn to be a person; before you learn a craft, learn
its ethics.* I found I agreed with it completely.

There is nothing new in the line, which is why I have filed it under "old
sayings, said again". But for about a year now, every evening as I walk home
down the same alley, the first half of it surfaces in my head — *before you
learn to do things, learn to be a person*. Perhaps it is because I am always a
little anxious in social settings, so I overthink after every event I attend.
Having heard, and said, a great deal about how "we do business models", "we do
market segments", "we do industry consolidation" — all these *ways of doing* —
I find myself having to remind myself again and again, over all that noise:
whatever you do, first be a decent person. My own reading of that comes down to
two things. First, keep the desire to possess in check. Second, find your own
line and defend it.

Everyone probably wants to possess something. Held badly, that want becomes the
source of a great deal of unhappiness. Within a family, an excess of
possessiveness can breach the last line of individual independence and produce
resentment that seems impossible to explain. That familiar phrase — *I'm doing
this for your own good* — is, to a large extent, "I" claiming rather too much
ownership over you.

In business I have been revising myself continually. I began cautious, terrified
of coming off worse in any exchange, and moved gradually towards open
collaboration. When it is necessary, I am now willing to take the smaller half
myself, as long as the objective gets met. The benefit of this is, first, that
things actually get done quickly. Second, and more importantly, my energy stops
going into fretting over gains and losses and goes into what matters. There is
a third benefit: playing down the wish to own something lets me judge it from a
more objective, more quantitative position. *The player at the board is the one
who cannot see it.* Once you let go of treating a thing as your own flesh and
blood, you can look at the position from outside it.

The coldness that the first principle produces has to be corrected, constantly,
by the second. The hardest part is discovering your own line — or, put
differently, your final objective. In building a company there are voices all
around you. Without much experience of my own, I am one of the blind men with
the elephant, and each voice describes some other part of the enormous animal
in front of me. What I need is to find the best available answer under
incomplete information. One way is to listen to more opinions and gather more
viewpoints. The other is to work through the logic behind those different
viewpoints and the relationships between them. There is a further difficulty:
the optimum I am after should be a global one, so across time I also have to
avoid one-sided judgements that trap me in a local optimum. And once you have
found the objective, you have to hold it against every temptation. Like the
foal crossing the river in the fable — having heard a great deal of advice, you
still have to go and find out for yourself.

So the two halves of "being a decent person" while building something, as I
understand it: find a suitable objective and a way of describing it, then go at
it boldly — but never let yourself sit in *my idea is unique* for so long that
self-admiration costs you your basic objectivity.
