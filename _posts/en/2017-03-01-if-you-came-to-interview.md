I have been looking for people continuously for a while now — found online,
introduced by friends, machine-learning researchers, technical and
non-technical roles — and I have spoken directly or indirectly with a great
many of them. Here is the recruiter's view of what tends to leave a poor
impression in an interview. These are personal opinions; I hope they help, and
I am happy to be argued with. (Everything below is void for anyone with an
unusual enough background.)

**First: the CV.** For me and most candidates, the CV is the first exchange we
have. So presenting one that is logically clear and plainly written really does
seem essential to a good first impression. List education simply. Describe your
professional background systematically. Where an experience stands out, explain
it in a little more detail. Avoid long sentences wherever you can, and use the
layout to separate the sections cleanly. I imagine you can read this advice in
many places — but it genuinely, genuinely matters. Once it is written, show it
to a friend with some visual taste and let them tidy it up. If you cannot find
one, then check it yourself: consistent indentation and alignment, one
typeface, no typos. As for a photograph, I personally think you can take it or
leave it; a professionally styled portrait is a little strange.

**Second: still the CV.** Before you come in, go back and reread what you wrote
on it. For anything within the last year, you should be able to state clearly
what led to what and why. For anything within the last six months, you should
be able to answer on the technical details. Your interviewer will certainly ask
carefully about whatever you have highlighted, so prepare it properly.
Otherwise you get the case where the answer to "what are you strongest at?" is
"linear algebra", followed by an inability to diagonalise a matrix. For my own
part, I do not put much weight on thinking on your feet, because plenty of
problems at work are ones I cannot solve on the spot either — I go and look
things up. So when a candidate tells me they prepared specifically for this
interview, I think that is a genuinely good sign.

**Third: understand the role, and the company you might be joining.** Some
companies — ours among them — have very little material online, and a lot of
the detail simply cannot be looked up. But if a candidate can tell me what they
did manage to find, and bring specific questions about the company and about
how the role relates to them, I am close to moved. How much you can say here,
and how valuable your questions are, is genuinely a matter of what you put in:
nobody can know us deeply, so the information you gather is more or less
proportional to the time you spend. When I was job-hunting I would usually write
a cover letter aimed at a particular company or role. Many positions no longer
ask for one, but you should still prepare to the standard of being able to
write it.

The rest really does come down to ability. The summary above is fairly general,
but broadly:

1. A concise, logical CV.
2. Know yourself (be fluent in your own CV) and know the other side (understand
   the role you are interviewing for).

I have kept it general on purpose, so that you can hold it against your own
situation and ask whether you have prepared enough.
