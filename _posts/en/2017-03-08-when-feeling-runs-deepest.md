Written on and off as the mood took me. Not necessarily right, and not
necessarily wrong.

Setting aside all the things I am bad at — socialising, moving investors, and
so on — the strongest feeling I have at the moment, and one of the hardest
parts of building a company for someone like me, is how to restrain my own
scattering ideas and keep the product's scope inside something reasonable.
Concretely, it comes down to the following, and I would very much like to
discuss them.

**1. Choosing a direction.** This is the first thing and the most important
thing. Talk to different people and you get different feedback. Is it SaaS,
CRO, R&D, or a platform? Is it technology-driven, PR-driven, or is traffic
king? Different users want different things: painstaking deep analysis, a
comprehensive integrated solution, vertically targeted technical support, a
general-purpose platform service. So what should we build, how, and on what
schedule?

My effort here so far has gone into one question: who is the core user? My own
definition of a core user is one you can reach quickly, who gives you a base to
iterate and expand from, and who fits the technical character of the team. The
first part means the technology you are researching cannot sit too far from the
market — which requires understanding precisely what this group needs, and in
what form they need it. The second means the market is large enough, and that
your early users are *the critical node in a complex network*, the *one hair
that moves the whole body*, or the entry point into the system. The last means
the first two are things the team is good at and actually wants to do —
otherwise you may as well go and find a good job. Once those are settled,
features follow from needs, plans follow from the logic of the market, and
everything else can wait.

**2. Building the team.** Given a fixed objective and the core team you
already have, how do you expand and arrange people for the highest efficiency?
Do you find partners with strong fundamentals and grow them, or bring in
experienced people to survey the ground for you? Possibly neither is the best
framing of the question. My answer now is that all of it rests on the analysis
in point 1. Under the market and business plan that point 1 fixes: what kind of
person do we need right now, what do we need in reserve for the next year, and
for three years, and for five? Which colleagues are decisive in that process —
people who can hold a whole area on their own, or who can open the door to
future talent, whether by developing it or by recruiting it? Hire fast for what
is urgent, and for the long-term core positions build a regular screening
process so that the right people surface.

My own mistake before was this: whenever I met someone impressive, I wanted to
find a way to work with them. I now understand more clearly that a startup has
to be built around point 1. If an impressive person does not match the
objective, they are not only a financial burden — they can pull the whole
direction off course. Being friends is perfectly good too. This, I suppose, is
what is meant by *a thousand soldiers are easy to find; one general is not*.

Of course, however NP-hard the problem is, there are excellent co-founders and
there are friends holding all of this up, so every day is painful and happy at
once.
